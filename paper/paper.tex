\begin{abstract}
Why can a temporary restriction on the use of property cost more output than an equal relaxation adds? I study an incumbent that knows its legal access will return after one period. As long as the firm operates, its optimal output is linear in usable property, so losses and gains are equal in size. A severe restriction can lead the firm to close, sacrificing the output it could still have produced. Free reopening restores production when access returns. If closure dismantles the organization and rebuilding costs more than the restored business is worth, the loss persists. Higher rebuilding costs can also keep the firm open through a milder restriction. This incentive stops growing once the cost reaches the restored business's value. Affordable suspension lets the firm preserve its organization and resume production when access returns.
\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}
Do countries lose more output after a deterioration in economic freedom than they gain after an equally large improvement? Figure~\ref{fig:thesis-irfs} reports estimates from my thesis using the Economic Freedom of the World (EFW) index \citep{lawson2025}. Each curve traces the cumulative response of real GDP per capita to a one-point EFW change in the same direction at two successive five-year observations. The left panel shows the gain under imposed symmetry, where the loss is its mirror image. The right estimates gains and losses separately. At ten years the estimates are $+\irfGainTen$ and $\irfDeteriorationTen$ log points. The loss exceeds the gain by $\irfGapTen$ log points, with $p=\irfTestTen$ in a pointwise test of equal magnitudes. At fifteen years the same test gives $p=\irfTestFifteen$. The evidence for unequal magnitudes is strongest at ten years.

The country-level estimates leave open how much of the loss comes from firms producing less and how much comes from firms shutting down. Consider an incumbent that can still produce during a restriction, but only at a loss. It can absorb that loss to keep its organization intact, or close and save today's expense. If closing disperses its workforce and supplier relationships, restored access to the same physical property may no longer be enough to resume production. The question is whether that one decision can make the loss both larger and longer-lived than the gain from an equal improvement.

The estimates come from my unpublished thesis, \emph{Choques de Libertad Económica: Evidencia Global de Efectos Asimétricos y No Lineales} (manuscript compiled July 30, 2026, Figure~2 and Table~2). The local projections use the Fraser Institute's EFW index and World Bank real GDP per capita at five-year intervals from 1970 to 2020. Each response combines the current and lagged coefficients and measures the cumulative change in log real GDP per capita from the preceding observation. Inner bands give 95\% country-clustered pointwise intervals. Outer bands give 95\% simultaneous sup-$t$ bands across horizons for each path.

In \citet{dixit1989}, an uncertain output price generates distinct thresholds for entry and exit when switching is costly. Here the price stays fixed and the incumbent knows exactly when legal access to its property will return. I compare a one-period restriction with an equal relaxation. While the firm operates, optimal output is linear in usable property, so the loss and gain in levels are equal. If it closes during the restriction, the loss exceeds the gain by exactly the output it could still have produced that period. Comparing free and costly reopening then shows how much of the loss survives restoration.

The same rebuilding cost can discourage closure today and prevent reopening tomorrow. The threshold in Proposition~\ref{prop:regions} explains why. If a restored business is worth $V$ and rebuilding costs $\kappa<V$, keeping the organization intact saves the firm $\kappa$ when access returns. Once $\kappa$ reaches $V$, further increases have no effect on today's decision. The most the firm can lose by closing is the entire restored business. Its incentive to stay open is therefore the discounted value of the smaller of $\kappa$ and $V$. A restriction large enough to overcome that incentive induces closure, making the impact loss exceed the gain. Conditional on closure, the profitability of rebuilding determines how long the loss lasts.

\section{Related literature}\label{sec:literature}
\citet[pp.~622--623]{dixit1989} gives the closest starting point. A firm that has already paid to enter a market can profitably continue at prices too low to justify entry by an idle firm. If a price decline is expected to reverse, an incumbent may even accept a temporary operating loss.

In the stochastic-price model of \citet[secs.~III--IV]{dixit1989}, an idle firm waits for a higher price to enter than an active firm requires to keep operating. Between the two thresholds, firms facing the same price can make different decisions because of their histories. Here the selling price stays fixed and the firm knows the date of restoration. By comparing equal restrictions and relaxations for the same incumbent, I can measure the excess loss exactly. It equals the output the firm could still have produced on the adverse path when it chose to close. Comparing free and costly rebuilding then isolates the portion of that loss that survives restoration.

What survives an interruption is a separate economic choice. \citet[pp.~119--120]{dixit1992} distinguishes temporary suspension from abandonment and analyzes the case in which closure immediately destroys the setup, so restarting requires the original investment again. \citet{fleten2017} distinguish shutdown, startup, and abandonment for U.S. electricity generators and find that greater regulatory uncertainty is associated with lower probabilities of both shutdown and startup. In the experiment here the firm already knows when normal conditions will return. Its reason to pay for preservation is the value of the organization it will have on that date.

Evidence from a different participation decision helps interpret that cost. \citet{roberts1997} use Colombian manufacturing plants' export histories to distinguish sunk costs of entering foreign markets from persistent differences in profitability. They find that prior exporting experience raises the probability of exporting again. Exiting a foreign market can leave a plant operating domestically. For the mechanism studied here, the corresponding evidence would have to follow the establishment itself and the resources it retained through a production shutdown.
\section{Model and maintained assumptions}\label{sec:model}
An incumbent owns a productive asset and enters the experiment with its organization in place. Legal access determines how much of the asset it can use. Its own activity decision determines whether it retains the organization needed to use the asset next period. The following assumptions specify these two parts of the firm's state.

\textbf{A1. Technology and prices.} Dates are $t=0,1,\ldots$. Output price is one. Fixed pre-existing property $\bar k>0$ is normalized to one. With $A>0$, $w>0$, and $0<\alpha<1$, operating output is $As_t^{1-\alpha}x_t^\alpha$. The usable share lies in $(0,1]$. Technology, prices, and physical property do not change across experiments.

\textbf{A2. Operating costs.} Ordinary overhead is $F>0$. Institutional overhead is constant, $c_t=c_0\geq0$, and both are paid only when operating. This experiment changes $s$, not $c$.

\textbf{A3. Feasible choices and state.} Activity $a_t\in\{0,1\}$ and input $x_t\geq0$ satisfy $x_t=0$ when $a_t=0$. Initially $a_{-1}=1$. Closure is free, has no salvage payment, preserves physical property, and dismantles the organization. Every closed-to-open transition costs the same $\kappa\geq0$. Inactivity has zero payoff. No temporary suspension preserves the organization in the main model.

\textbf{A4. Financing and information.} Financing is available for inputs, operating losses, and restarting. There is no wealth state, capital accumulation, or effect of today's input on tomorrow's opportunities. The firm knows the entire institutional path and maximizes discounted profit with $0<\beta<1$.

\textbf{A5. Matched shock paths.} For $j\in\{-,0,+\}$, date-zero usable property is $s_0-d,s_0,s_0+d$, respectively, with $0<d<s_0$ and $s_0+d\leq1$. Every path returns permanently to $s_0$ at date one. All other primitives are fixed.

\textbf{A6. Normal viability and scope.} Optimized normal operating profit $\pi_0$ is strictly positive. The comparison concerns the same incumbent. Potential entrants, endogenous market prices, and reallocation are outside its choice set.

At the start of date $t$, the firm inherits its previous activity $a_{t-1}$ and faces the usable share $s_t^j$ on path $j$. It chooses whether to operate and, if it operates, how much variable input to buy. It earns the resulting payoff that date and carries today's activity into the next date. Thus $s_0$ in A5 denotes the \emph{normal} usable share, while $s_t^j$ denotes the share actually available on a given date and path. Each path starts with the same incumbent.

Suppressing the path superscript for readability, the firm solves
\begin{equation}\label{eq:full}
\max_{\{a_t,x_t\}}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta^t
\left[As_t^{1-\alpha}x_t^\alpha-wx_t-(F+c_0)a_t
-\kappa a_t(1-a_{t-1})\right].
\end{equation}
Revenue and the input bill vanish during closure because feasibility requires $x_t=0$. The product $a_t(1-a_{t-1})$ equals one only when the firm operates today after being inactive yesterday. Thus an incumbent that stays open pays overhead but owes no rebuilding cost, and an inactive firm owes $\kappa$ only on the date it reopens. The original setup expenditure is sunk. I compare this problem at $\kappa=0$ and at a positive $\kappa$, holding the other primitives fixed.

\section{Input choice and continuation values}\label{sec:values}
The solution starts inside an operating period. Once the optimal input is known for each usable share, the cash flow from any activity path is determined. The value of being active or inactive after restoration then determines the two options available during the shock.

\begin{lemma}[Operating optimum]\label{lem:input}
Under A1--A2, conditional on operating at $s>0$, the unique global optimum satisfies
\begin{align}
\alpha As^{1-\alpha}x^{\alpha-1}&=w,\label{eq:foc}\\
x^*(s)&=s(\alpha A/w)^{1/(1-\alpha)},\\
q(s)&=sA(\alpha A/w)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)},\\
\pi(s,c_0)&=(1-\alpha)q(s)-F-c_0.\label{eq:profit}
\end{align}
The input FOC is independent of the restart cost.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Fix a usable share $s>0$ and suppose the firm operates. Its profit as a function of input is $As^{1-\alpha}x^\alpha-wx-F-c_0$. For $x>0$ its slope and curvature are
\[
 \alpha As^{1-\alpha}x^{\alpha-1}-w,\qquad
 \alpha(\alpha-1)As^{1-\alpha}x^{\alpha-2}<0.
\]
Because $0<\alpha<1$, the slope falls continuously from $+\infty$ near zero to $-w$ at large $x$. Profit therefore rises from the boundary $x=0$, reaches a unique peak, and then falls. This establishes that the first-order condition selects the global maximizer.

At the peak the marginal revenue product equals the input price $w$. Multiplying~\eqref{eq:foc} by $(x^*)^{1-\alpha}$ and dividing by $w$ yields the first equality below. Taking both sides to the power $1/(1-\alpha)>0$ gives the optimal purchase.
\[
 (x^*)^{1-\alpha}=\frac{\alpha A}{w}s^{1-\alpha}
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 x^*=s\left(\frac{\alpha A}{w}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)} .
\]
Substituting the optimal purchase into the production function gives
\[
 q(s)=A s^{1-\alpha}
        \left[s\left(\frac{\alpha A}{w}\right)^{1/(1-\alpha)}\right]^\alpha
      =s A\left(\frac{\alpha A}{w}\right)^{\alpha/(1-\alpha)} .
\]
The exponents on $s$ add to one because the firm scales its purchased input with the property it can use. Multiplying both sides of~\eqref{eq:foc} by the optimal input gives
\[
 w x^*(s)=\alpha A s^{1-\alpha}[x^*(s)]^\alpha=\alpha q(s).
\]
At the optimum, the input bill absorbs the fraction $\alpha$ of revenue. The remaining $(1-\alpha)q(s)$ pays the two fixed expenses and leaves the profit in~\eqref{eq:profit}. A restart payment is fixed with respect to $x$, and A4 makes future opportunities independent of $x$. The same input choice therefore applies on a reopening date.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{From input choice to the value of an activity path.}
Fix a shock path $j$ and any feasible sequence of activity decisions. Choosing $x^*(s_t^j)$ at every operating date and zero otherwise gives the highest feasible period payoff at every date. Input affects neither the inherited activity next period nor the dates on which the firm must pay $\kappa$. Write $\pi_t^j=\pi(s_t^j,c_0)$. The remaining choice is therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reduced}
\max_{a_t\in\{0,1\}}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\beta^t
\left[a_t\pi_t^j-\kappa a_t(1-a_{t-1})\right].
\end{equation}
To see why this replacement is valid over an infinite horizon, let $R_t$ denote its period payoff on the chosen activity path. Let $r_t$ be the payoff under any other feasible inputs on the same path. Lemma~\ref{lem:input} gives a nonnegative shortfall $g_t=R_t-r_t$. For each finite $T$,
\[
 \sum_{t=0}^{T}\beta^t r_t
  =\sum_{t=0}^{T}\beta^t R_t-\sum_{t=0}^{T}\beta^t g_t .
\]
There are only three possible usable shares, so optimized profits have a finite bound $|\pi_t^j|\leq C$. The first sum on the right converges absolutely since $|R_t|\leq C+\kappa$ and $\beta<1$. The second increases to a finite value or to $+\infty$. Hence any feasible inputs give a discounted payoff weakly below the optimized one, including when their payoff is $-\infty$. The proposed inputs make every $g_t$ zero and attain the bound. Taking the best activity path gives the same value in~\eqref{eq:full} and~\eqref{eq:reduced}.

\paragraph{What an intact organization is worth.}
Write $q_0=q(s_0)>0$ for normal output and $\pi_0=\pi(s_0,c_0)>0$ for normal operating profit. Let $\pi_\pm=\pi(s_0\pm d,c_0)$ denote the two date-zero operating profits. From date one onward, every path has the same usable property and normal profit. Define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:V}V=\frac{\pi_0}{1-\beta}>0.\end{equation}
\begin{lemma}[Restored-state values and delayed restarting]\label{lem:value}
Under A1--A6, at any restored date the value of an active firm is $V$, and that of an inactive firm is $W=\max\{V-\kappa,0\}$. Immediate restart is uniquely optimal if $V>\kappa$; permanent inactivity is uniquely optimal if $V<\kappa$. At equality, any restart date and never restarting give the same value.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Measure value at a date on which normal access has already returned. An active firm can collect $\pi_0$ immediately and in every subsequent period. The value of doing so is $\sum_{m=0}^\infty\beta^m\pi_0=\pi_0/(1-\beta)=V$. Any path that closes for even one period sacrifices a strictly positive discounted profit and may add a nonnegative restart payment. Remaining active is therefore uniquely optimal.

Now suppose the organization has already been dismantled. A feasible path either stays closed forever or has a first restart after some finite delay $n\geq0$. Once the firm restarts, the active-firm argument just proved applies. The best path with that first restart earns zero before $n$, pays $\kappa$ at $n$, and earns $\pi_0$ from $n$ onward. Its value today is
\[
 -\beta^n\kappa+\sum_{m=n}^{\infty}\beta^m\pi_0
 =\beta^n\left(-\kappa+\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\beta^r\pi_0\right)
 =\beta^n(V-\kappa).
\]
If $V>\kappa$, delaying discounts a positive net payoff, so $n=0$ strictly dominates every delay and permanent inactivity. If $V<\kappa$, waiting makes the loss smaller in present value, but each finite restart still loses money. Permanent inactivity attains zero. If $V=\kappa$, every first restart date followed by uninterrupted operation gives zero, as does staying closed forever. Maximizing over these alternatives gives $W=\max\{V-\kappa,0\}$. The argument covers all feasible paths because closing again after a first restart would forgo positive profits.
\end{proof}
\section{Output after a temporary deterioration}\label{sec:impact}
Return to date zero, when the incumbent still has its organization. Operating earns the current profit $\pi_j$ and carries an active firm worth $V$ into date one. Closing yields zero today and carries an inactive firm worth $W$ into date one. At the date of the decision the two alternatives are worth
\[
 \underbrace{\pi_j+\beta V}_{\text{operate through the shock}}
 \qquad\text{and}\qquad
 \underbrace{\beta W}_{\text{close and retain the option to restart}} .
\]
Both future values are discounted once because the comparison is made today. In particular, closure can have positive value even though it yields no current revenue.

Let $y_t^j=a_t^jq(s_t^j)$ denote actual output. On each date compare the adverse and favorable paths with the same no-shock path. Write $L_t=y_t^0-y_t^-$ for the size of the loss and $G_t=y_t^+-y_t^0$ for the size of the gain.
\begin{proposition}[Persistent strict asymmetry]\label{prop:main}
Maintain A1--A6. Suppose
\begin{equation}\label{eq:conditions}
\pi_0>0,\qquad \pi_-+\beta V<0,\qquad \kappa>V.
\end{equation}
Then the unique optimal activity paths are $a_t^-=0$ and $a_t^0=a_t^+=1$ for every $t\geq0$. Consequently
\begin{align}
L_0=q_0&>G_0=(d/s_0)q_0,\\
L_t=q_0&>G_t=0\qquad(t\geq1).
\end{align}
For the otherwise identical free-restart baseline, the adverse firm closes at date zero and reopens at date one; the unchanged and favorable firms remain active. Impact losses and gains are the same as above, but $L_t=G_t=0$ for $t\geq1$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Choose activity on each path.} Since $\kappa>V$, Lemma~\ref{lem:value} gives $W=0$. The adverse firm strictly prefers zero from closing to $\pi_-+\beta V<0$ from operating. It subsequently remains inactive because every finite restart would lose money. The unaffected firm can earn $\pi_0+\beta V=V>0$ by operating. Higher usable property raises operating profit by Lemma~\ref{lem:input}, so $\pi_++\beta V>V$ and the favorable firm also operates. Normal profits are positive from date one onward, making continued operation optimal for both.

\emph{Convert activity into output.} Linearity gives $q(s_0\pm d)=q_0(1\pm d/s_0)$. At impact the favorable firm produces $q_0(1+d/s_0)$, the unaffected firm produces $q_0$, and the closed firm produces zero. Subtracting the no-shock benchmark in the appropriate direction gives
\[
 G_0=q_0(1+d/s_0)-q_0=(d/s_0)q_0,\qquad L_0=q_0-0=q_0.
\]
The gain is strictly smaller because $d/s_0<1$. After restoration the favorable and unaffected firms both produce $q_0$, while the closed firm produces zero. Hence $G_t=0$ and $L_t=q_0$ for every $t\geq1$.

\emph{Repeat the decision with free rebuilding.} Setting $\kappa=0$ gives $W=V$. Both choices now deliver the same future value $\beta V$, so the advantage of operating today is exactly $\pi_j$. The hypothesis $\pi_-+\beta V<0$ implies $\pi_-<0$, while $\pi_0$ and $\pi_+$ are positive. Only the adverse firm closes, just as in the costly case. Once access returns, restarting immediately gives it $V>0$ and its output returns to $q_0$. The two reopening regimes therefore have identical impact losses and gains, but different losses after restoration.
\end{proof}

Panel A of Figure~\ref{fig:impact-recovery} shows where the excess loss comes from. Had the firm stayed open, the restriction would have cost it $q_0-q(s_0-d)=0.32$ units, exactly the gain from an equal improvement. Closing also sacrifices the $q(s_0-d)=0.48$ units it could still have produced. Panel B follows the firm after access returns. With free reopening it recovers the entire $0.80$ units. With costly reopening the entire loss persists, now because the firm remains closed even though it can use its property again.

\section{Switching regions and the strength of the conditions}\label{sec:thresholds}
Proposition~\ref{prop:main} studies a severe restriction and an expensive restart. For a milder restriction, even a firm with negative current profit may keep operating. The relevant comparison is the cash loss it avoids by closing against the value of bringing an intact organization into the next period.
\begin{samepage}
\begin{proposition}[Complete closure threshold and weak asymmetry]\label{prop:regions}
Under A1--A6, for any $\kappa\geq0$ the adverse firm strictly prefers closure at date zero if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:threshold}
(1-\alpha)q_0\frac{d}{s_0}>\pi_0+\beta\min\{\kappa,V\}.
\end{equation}
The reverse strict inequality implies continued operation; equality gives indifference. If it closes, $\kappa<V$ implies reopening at date one and $\kappa>V$ implies permanent inactivity; $\kappa=V$ gives the indifference described in Lemma~\ref{lem:value}. Under any optimal selection at ties, $L_t\geq G_t\geq0$ for every $t$. At impact strictness occurs exactly when the adverse firm closes; after restoration it occurs exactly when that firm remains closed.
\end{proposition}
\end{samepage}
\begin{proof}
First compute how much the restriction takes out of current profit. Usable property and optimized output both fall by the fraction $d/s_0$. Fixed operating expenses are the same on both paths, so~\eqref{eq:profit} gives
\[
 \pi_0-\pi_-=(1-\alpha)[q_0-q(s_0-d)]
             =(1-\alpha)q_0\,d/s_0 .
\]
Next value what the firm gives up at the restoration date if it closes today. With the organization intact it has $V$. Once closed it has $W=\max\{V-\kappa,0\}$. Substituting for $W$ in each case gives
\[
 V-W=
 \begin{cases}
  V-(V-\kappa)=\kappa, & 0\leq\kappa<V,\\
  V-0=V, & \kappa\geq V .
 \end{cases}
\]
In the first branch, staying open saves the expense of a profitable restart. In the second, closing gives up the restored business. At $\kappa=V$ the values of the two expressions coincide. Hence the date-zero value of preserving the organization is $\beta\min\{\kappa,V\}$.

Finally subtract the closure payoff from the operating payoff. On the adverse path the difference is
\begin{align*}
 (\pi_-+\beta V)-\beta W
   &=\pi_-+\beta(V-W)\\
   &=\pi_0-(1-\alpha)q_0\,d/s_0+\beta\min\{\kappa,V\}.
\end{align*}
Closure is strictly preferred when this difference is negative. Moving the lost operating margin to the other side gives~\eqref{eq:threshold}. Equality makes both actions optimal. On the unchanged and favorable paths, $\pi_j>0$ and $V-W\geq0$, so operating strictly dominates closure.

It remains to check losses and gains, including at a tie. If the adverse firm operates, linearity gives $L_0=G_0=(d/s_0)q_0$. If it closes, $L_0=q_0>G_0$. After restoration $G_t=0$ because normal and favorable output coincide. At any such date the adverse firm either produces $q_0$, giving $L_t=0$, or is inactive, giving $L_t=q_0$. Lemma~\ref{lem:value} specifies when each action is optimal and allows every restart date at $\kappa=V$. Thus the inequality holds for each optimal choice individually.
\end{proof}
\paragraph{How thin must the normal profit margin be?}
For permanent closure to be uniquely optimal, rebuilding must cost more than $V$ and the initial restriction must be severe enough to make the firm surrender that value. When $\kappa>V$, the right-hand side of~\eqref{eq:threshold} is $\pi_0+\beta V=V$. Substitute $V=\pi_0/(1-\beta)$ and multiply the inequality by $1-\beta>0$. This gives $(1-\beta)(1-\alpha)q_0d/s_0>\pi_0$. Dividing by the positive contribution to revenue after variable input cost, $(1-\alpha)q_0$, yields the joint conditions
\[
\boxed{\quad 0<\frac{\pi_0}{(1-\alpha)q_0}<(1-\beta)\frac d{s_0}
       \qquad\text{and}\qquad \kappa>\frac{\pi_0}{1-\beta}.\quad}
\]
The ratio on the left is the fraction of normal revenue net of variable costs that remains after both fixed operating expenses. A thin margin makes the business viable while it is open, yet leaves little future profit to protect during a restriction or to cover a new setup payment after closure. The two inequalities answer different questions. The first determines whether the owner gives up its existing organization under the restriction. The second determines whether it would buy that organization back under normal conditions.

\section{Numerical experiments}\label{sec:numerical}
The following example works from the primitives to the firm's decisions. Set $A=2$, $\alpha=1/2$, $w=1.25$, $F=0.30$, $c_0=0.09$, $s_0=0.50$, and $\beta=0.90$. Lemma~\ref{lem:input} gives $x^*(s)=0.64s$, $q(s)=1.60s$, and $\pi(s,c_0)=0.80s-0.39$. Under normal access the firm buys $0.32$ units of input and produces $0.80$. Half of that revenue pays for the variable input. Fixed expenses absorb $0.39$ of the remaining $0.40$, leaving a normal profit of $0.01$ and a restored business worth $V=0.01/(1-0.90)=0.10$. All quantities use model units, and a period has no assigned calendar length.

Take a restriction of $d=0.20$ and a rebuilding cost of $\kappa=0.40$. The normal profit margin in Section~\ref{sec:thresholds} is $0.01/0.40=2.5\%$, while the cutoff is $(1-0.90)(0.20/0.50)=4\%$. Rebuilding also costs more than the restored value of $0.10$. Both strict conditions hold throughout a neighborhood of this example because the underlying values vary continuously with the primitives.

\subsection*{How the same rebuilding cost affects closure and recovery}
Read Table~\ref{tab:cases} in pairs, holding the size of the restriction fixed. With $d=0.10$, the firm can still produce $0.64$ but would lose $0.07$ by operating this period. Preserving the organization gives it $\beta V=0.09$ in discounted future profits, so the total value of continuing is $-0.07+0.09=0.02$. With free rebuilding, closure also preserves those future profits and avoids today's loss. Closing is worth $0.09$ and wins. With $\kappa=0.40$, closing is worth zero. The incumbent now accepts the same current loss because operating is the only way to retain the future profits.

With $d=0.20$, the adverse operating profit falls to $-0.15$. Even after adding $0.09$ in future profits, continuing is worth $-0.06$. Closure wins in both rows. The two decisions diverge at restoration, when rebuilding for free yields $0.10$ but paying $0.40$ would buy a business worth only $0.10$. The zero in the last row at date one therefore has a different explanation from the zero at date zero.

For admissible shocks below the line, operating wins. Above it, closing wins. The cutoff rises from $0.0125$ with free reopening to $0.125$ at $\kappa=V=0.10$. Beyond that cost, a closed firm already has zero value, so a further increase cannot make closure any less attractive. Moving horizontally across the map at $d=0.10$ changes the initial decision. Moving across at $d=0.20$ changes only the decision after closure.

\subsection*{Sensitivity to the discount factor}
Greater patience makes future profits more valuable. To isolate this effect, Table~\ref{tab:discount} holds $d=0.20$ and $\kappa=0.40$ fixed. For every discount factor shown, $V<\kappa$, so closure continues to be worth zero. The firm breaks even by operating when its current loss exactly absorbs the discounted value of the restored business. Substituting $V=\pi_0/(1-\beta)$ and multiplying by the positive denominator gives
\[
  0=\pi_-+\frac{\beta\pi_0}{1-\beta}
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  0=(1-\beta)\pi_-+\beta\pi_0
  =\pi_-+\beta(\pi_0-\pi_-).
\]
Since $\pi_0-\pi_-=0.16>0$, the cutoff is $\beta^*=-\pi_-/(\pi_0-\pi_-)=0.15/0.16=0.9375$. At the cutoff, operating throughout and closing permanently are both optimal. At $\beta=0.95$ the restored business is worth $0.20$. Continuing is therefore worth $-0.15+0.95(0.20)=0.04$, and the firm stays open. Its current loss and the price of rebuilding are the same as in the first row. What has changed is the present value of keeping the organization.

\section{Robustness and aggregation}
\subsection*{What if the firm can preserve the organization without producing?}
In the main model the firm must produce to retain its organization. This makes preserving it expensive during a severe restriction. Suppose instead that the incumbent can pay a one-period expense $h$ to keep the organization available while production is suspended. This third action separates today's production decision from tomorrow's ability to resume.

\begin{proposition}[Cheap suspension removes persistence]\label{prop:suspension}
Maintain A1--A6 and the strict conditions of Proposition~\ref{prop:main}, except allow an active firm to suspend at date zero, produce nothing, pay a total preservation expense $h\geq0$ instead of operating overhead, and retain its organization for costless resumption at date one. If $h<\beta V$, the adverse firm uniquely suspends and resumes at date one. Normal and favorable firms operate. Impact loss remains $q_0$, but losses and gains both vanish from date one.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Suspending produces no revenue today and costs $h$ in place of operating expenses. The firm still enters the restored period with its organization intact, so it receives the same continuation value $V$ as a firm that operated. At the date of the restriction, the three payoffs on the adverse path are
\[
 \underbrace{\pi_-+\beta V}_{\text{operate}}<0,\qquad
 \underbrace{\beta W}_{\text{close}}=0,\qquad
 \underbrace{-h+\beta V}_{\text{suspend}}>0
 \quad\text{when }h<\beta V .
\]
The first two comparisons follow from Proposition~\ref{prop:main}. The third follows by subtracting the preservation expense from the discounted value of the intact business. Hence suspension is uniquely optimal. At restoration the firm can resume without paying $\kappa$ and earns the strictly positive normal profit from then on.

On the normal and favorable paths, operation and suspension both preserve the organization. Operating earns $\pi_j+h>0$ more than suspending and also dominates closure. Only the adverse firm suspends, giving $L_0=q_0$. At restoration all three paths produce $q_0$, so $L_t=G_t=0$. On the adverse path, closure and suspension tie at zero when $h=\beta V$ and imply different recoveries. For $h>\beta V$, that firm strictly prefers closure to either alternative.
\end{proof}
In Figure~\ref{fig:suspension}, the firm chooses the highest of three payoffs at each preservation cost. At the marked cost of $h=0.02$, suspension buys $0.09$ in discounted future profits for a net payoff of $0.07$. Closing pays zero and operating pays $-0.06$. Each additional unit spent on preservation lowers only the suspension payoff. When $h$ reaches $0.09$, the owner is indifferent even though suspension restores next period's output to $0.80$ and closure leaves it at zero.

\subsection*{What carries over to positive aggregate output?}
A closed firm's output is zero, so its logarithm is undefined. To compare the model with a log output measure, hold fixed a cohort of $N$ identical incumbents and add the output $B$ produced elsewhere. This keeps total output positive even when every incumbent in the cohort closes.

\begin{corollary}[Positive totals and logs]\label{cor:logs}
Under Proposition~\ref{prop:main}, suppose $Y_t^j=B+Ny_t^j$ with the same $B>0$ and $N>0$ in all paths. Then $Y_t^j>0$ and
\[
\log Y_t^0-\log Y_t^->\log Y_t^+-\log Y_t^0
\quad\text{for every }t\geq0.
\]
The same comparison applies to per-capita totals if population is identical across paths.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
First compare totals in levels. Subtracting cancels the common $B$, so $Y_t^0-Y_t^-=NL_t$ and $Y_t^+-Y_t^0=NG_t$. Since $N>0$ and Proposition~\ref{prop:main} gives $L_t>G_t$, the baseline total exceeds the arithmetic mean of the other two totals. All three totals are positive because $B>0$. The arithmetic--geometric mean inequality then gives
\[
 Y_t^0>\frac{Y_t^-+Y_t^+}{2}\ \geq\ \sqrt{Y_t^-Y_t^+}.
\]
Squaring both sides and taking the increasing logarithm yields $2\log Y_t^0>\log Y_t^-+\log Y_t^+$. Moving one copy of $\log Y_t^0$ and the adverse log total to the opposite sides gives the stated comparison. Dividing each total by the same population at date $t$ subtracts the same log population from every term in each difference, leaving the result unchanged.
\end{proof}
Curvature alone can generate a larger loss in logs. Take the same technology and $\kappa=0.40$ but a smaller shock, $d=0.05$, below the closure threshold. The incumbent stays active on all three paths and produces $0.72$, $0.80$, and $0.88$ at impact. With $B=2$ and $N=3$ the corresponding totals are $4.16$, $4.40$, and $4.64$. Relative to $4.40$, the loss and gain both equal $0.24$ units. In log points, however, $\log(4.40/4.16)\simeq0.0561$ exceeds $\log(4.64/4.40)\simeq0.0531$. The logarithm is steeper at the lower output level. Attributing the larger downside response in Figure~\ref{fig:thesis-irfs} to closures would require independent evidence on firm activity.

\section{Economic interpretation and empirical implications}
\paragraph{Observing closure and suspension.}
An establishment producing $(0,q_0,q_0,\ldots)$ could have followed either of two histories. With free reopening (Proposition~\ref{prop:main}), it dismantled its organization and rebuilt it when access returned. With affordable suspension (Proposition~\ref{prop:suspension}), it paid to preserve the organization during the interruption. Both histories produce the same recovery.

Linked payroll and licensing records could show whether key workers stayed attached and permits remained current during the interruption. Rehiring, relicensing, or spending to rebuild supplier relationships upon reopening could point to dismantling. The records are most informative together because a retained permit says little about the workforce.

Reopening rates alone miss the incumbents that absorbed losses to stay open. Tracking all initially active firms with comparable pre-shock margins and exposure would capture that decision as well. Access to financing also matters because A4 allows the incumbent to absorb a loss whenever preservation pays. Finally, a finite observation window gives only a lower bound on the duration of an unfinished closure. Permanent inactivity in the model relies on conditions staying normal after restoration.

\paragraph{From incumbents to aggregate output.}
Corollary~\ref{cor:logs} fixes the cohort and all other output. New establishments could restore aggregate production while the original firms remain idle. For an initially inactive owner with its own property and the same setup cost, entering on the favorable path and continuing is worth $\pi_++\beta V-\kappa$. This can be positive even when $\kappa>V$ makes rebuilding after restoration unprofitable. Establishment identifiers and output weights would help distinguish returning incumbents, new entrants, and other producers that expanded.

\section{Conclusion}
Restored legal access makes normal production feasible again. Whether production resumes depends on what happened to the organization during the restriction. A firm facing a modest loss can find it worthwhile to stay open to avoid rebuilding. A sufficiently severe loss induces closure even when reopening is free. Once closed, the firm remains idle if rebuilding costs more than the restored business is worth. The suspension experiment identifies what makes that loss persistent. An affordable way to retain the organization restores output as soon as legal access returns.

This mechanism has an observable implication. Among comparable establishments that stopped producing during the same restriction, those that retained workers, permits, and supplier relationships should resume more readily when rebuilding is expensive. If establishments that dismantled those relationships routinely reopened just as quickly and at little expense, costly rebuilding would be a weak explanation for persistent losses in that setting. Following the same establishments through restoration would test whether this mechanism helps explain the GDP responses in Figure~\ref{fig:thesis-irfs}.
