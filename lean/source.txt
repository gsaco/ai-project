                                                Abstract

         Can a temporary institutional deterioration cause a larger and more persistent output loss
     than the gain from an equal improvement? I study an initially active firm with Cobb–Douglas
     production, institutionally usable property, recurring institutional overhead, and a sunk restart
     cost. Equal property-use shocks produce symmetric output changes conditional on operating.
     Closure creates impact asymmetry; costly restarting can prevent recovery. I prove a conditional
     permanent-loss result, derive the complete switching comparison, and show how cheap suspen-
     sion eliminates persistence. Numerical illustrations check the algebra and decisions; they are
     not an empirical calibration. Formalization coverage and the author’s independent handwritten
     check are reported separately and must not be inferred from numerical success.


1    Question, interpretation, and contribution

My thesis studies real GDP per capita after changes in economic freedom. This paper develops one
firm-level mechanism behind the direction of the estimated asymmetry. The institutional variables
below are economic conditions, not Economic Freedom of the World (EFW) index points. Usable
property captures restrictions on productive use; institutional overhead captures recurring fixed
expenses. Neither variable exhausts the EFW framework.
Dixit (1989) studies entry and exit with sunk costs under uncertainty. Here institutional restoration
is known: waiting brings no new information. I retain the distinction between an incumbent and
an inactive owner, and derive a matched loss–gain comparison. Sunk-cost persistence is established
theory, not a novelty claim. Dixit (1992) distinguishes suspension from abandonment, motivating
the first robustness exercise. The source search and unavailable materials are documented in
proposal/literature-check.md.
The contribution to my thesis is a transparent conditional mechanism. The central result changes
the free-restart baseline’s full recovery into persistent inactivity. It does not identify an EFW
coefficient or prove that institutional deteriorations always dominate improvements in aggregate
data.
Research status. The results below have analytic proofs. Simulations check finite illustrations
and parameter grids. Independent author adjudication and complete source-to-Lean validation are
separate tasks; their actual status is recorded in the final-presentation materials.

Lemma 2 (Restored-state values and delayed restarting). Under A1–A6, at any restored date the
value of an active firm is V , and that of an inactive firm is W = max{V − κ, 0}. Immediate restart
is uniquely optimal if V > κ; permanent inactivity is uniquely optimal if V < κ. At equality, any

Proof. An active firm earns π0 each period by remaining active, giving the convergent geometric
series V . Closing forgoes strictly positive profits and can add a nonnegative restart payment; it
cannot improve this path. For a closed firm, any path with a first restart at date n ≥ 0 is weakly
improved by remaining active thereafter. Its value is then β n (V − κ). Never restarting gives zero.
If V − κ > 0, n = 0 strictly dominates all positive delays and never restarting. If V − κ < 0,
every finite delay gives a negative value and never restarting attains zero. If it is zero, all these
alternatives tie. This covers arbitrary reopening sequences, not only a finite simulation horizon.

Proof. Lemma 2 and κ > V give W = 0. Closing at date zero therefore has value zero. Adverse

Lemma 1 makes output linear in s, so q(s0 + d) = q0 (1 + d/s0 ). Date-zero output is zero under
deterioration, q0 under no change, and q0 (1 + d/s0 ) under improvement. Subtract to obtain the
first comparison; strictness uses 0 < d < s0 . Later the adverse output remains zero while the other
two outputs equal q0 .
For the baseline, set κ = 0. Then W = V and closing is worth βV , while continuing is worth
π− + βV . The assumed inequality implies π− < 0, so closing strictly dominates. Lemma 2 gives
immediate reopening at date one because V > 0. The other paths stay open because their date-zero
operating profits are positive. Thus restarting costs change recovery, not the existence of impact
asymmetry in this experiment.

The conditions do not assert an outcome as an assumption: each inequality compares values de-
rived from the primitive optimization problem. Positive normal profits are compatible with an
unprofitable restart because the incumbent has already paid its original setup cost. No conclusion
about entry by other owners is implied by κ > V .

7    Robustness and aggregation

Proposition 3 (Cheap suspension removes persistence). Maintain A1–A6 and the strict conditions
of Proposition 1, except allow an active firm to suspend at date zero, produce nothing, pay a total
preservation expense h ≥ 0 instead of operating overhead, and retain its organization for costless
resumption at date one. If h < βV , the adverse firm uniquely suspends and resumes at date one.
Normal and favorable firms operate. Impact loss remains q0 , but losses and gains both vanish from
date one.

Proof. Adverse continuation is negative and closure has value zero. Suspension is worth −h+βV >
0, so it strictly dominates both. On restoration, positive normal profit makes resumption optimal.
Normal and favorable operation yields πj + βV > −h + βV because πj > 0 and h ≥ 0, and
also exceeds closure. Subtract outputs to obtain the result. At h = βV , suspension and closure
tie; above that threshold, under these maintained strict conditions, closure is preferred. In the
illustration h = 0.02 gives suspension value 0.07, compared with −0.06 for operation and zero for
closure.

This is a fixed-cohort accounting comparison, not a model of GDP. A closed firm’s log output is
undefined. Even symmetric nonzero level changes can be asymmetric in logs; log curvature alone
must not be attributed to restart costs.
Entry and heterogeneity. Neither is solved here. The condition κ > V excludes this incumbent’s
later restart, not favorable date-zero entry by another owner: such entry has value π+ + βV − κ.
Excluding it would require further assumptions or the stronger cost restriction. Heterogeneous firms
may cross different thresholds; aggregation must account for their weights, unaffected production,
entry, prices, and reallocation. These are research questions, not results of the current simulations.

The proofs of Lemmas 1–2, Propositions 1–3, and Corollary 1 are complete as mathematical argu-
ments under the numbered assumptions. The delicate step is Lemma 2: closure retains an option
to reopen, and arbitrarily delayed or repeated switching must be covered. The finite simulations
alone cannot establish it.
Formal verification is a separate claim. The formalization run uses a pinned GitHub commit of
this paper, the AppliedModelingLib procedure, and a paper-scoped check. The exact source hash,

9    Questions for discussion
1. What evidence could distinguish organization-destroying closure from cheap suspension after an
   institutional deterioration?
2. Which minimal aggregation structure would preserve the firm-level comparison once entry and
   reallocation are allowed?
3. Are low normal profit margins and a known one-period institutional reversal the most informative
   starting point, or should restoration duration be the next extension?


10     Conclusion

The model makes a precise conditional statement. Input choices have a standard unchanged FOC.
Equal property-use shocks have equal and opposite effects while a firm operates. When the adverse
shock makes continuation unattractive and rebuilding exceeds the restored business value, the
adverse firm exits permanently. Free restarting restores production; cheap suspension can do so
too. The mechanism’s empirical relevance turns on whether institutional setbacks actually destroy
organizations and on how other firms respond.
